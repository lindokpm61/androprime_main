Andropause, often called the male menopause, is the popular name for the cluster of symptoms some men notice as testosterone slowly declines with age. The decline is real. But it isn't a single switch-off like the female menopause, and a symptom list on its own can't tell you whether testosterone is your problem. A blood test can show you where yours sits.

## Is the "male menopause" actually real?

He was forty-eight. Tuesday, like every Tuesday: alarm at six, out of the house by half seven, the nine o'clock meeting he'd chaired for six years. He got through it. He'd been getting through them for about two years.

Then at half two he sat in his car in the office car park, because he couldn't face the stairs back up to his desk.

A fortnight before that he'd typed "male menopause" into his phone at one in the morning, half-hoping it was a real thing, because at least then it would have a name. Two GPs had told him it was his age.

So is it a real thing?

Yes, in that testosterone falls as men age. The drop is slow and steady, about 1% a year from around the age of 30 to 40 (NHS, [The 'male menopause'](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/male-menopause/)). Over a decade that adds up. Plenty of men in their 40s are running lower than they did at 30 and feeling the difference.

No, in that it's nothing like the female menopause. There's no single switch-off moment. The NHS is blunt about it: "male menopause" is an unhelpful term, because it suggests a sudden hormone crash in middle age, and that isn't what happens. In many cases the symptoms have nothing to do with hormones at all (NHS, [The 'male menopause'](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/male-menopause/)).

Some men call it the manopause. I'm not sure andropause is a much better word, honestly. It's the one men type into their phones at one in the morning, so it's the one I'll use.

It isn't that the male menopause is a myth. It's that "menopause" is the wrong word for a slow, uneven change that may or may not be about testosterone at all.

## What andropause actually feels like

He hadn't gone looking for a number. He'd gone looking for a word that covered it.

The list is short and most men recognise it instantly. Energy that's gone by the afternoon. A sex drive that's quieter than it was. Sleep that doesn't put you back together. Recovery from training or a heavy week that takes longer than it used to. Mood that's flatter and harder to lift. Some men also notice more weight around the middle and weaker focus.

Closest thing to it: driving with the handbrake half on. The car still goes. You just work harder for the same speed, and you can't see anything wrong from the outside.

That's the bit that makes men wait. Nothing on that list is dramatic enough to book an appointment about, and all of it is enough to change your week.

If any of that is close to your last few months, the next part matters more than the label does. Low energy, low mood, poor sleep, a softer sex drive: these can be age-related testosterone change, or they can be sleep, stress, or lifestyle. A symptom list can't tell you which. A blood test can tell you where your testosterone actually sits.

### Why a symptom list can't tell you the cause

Every andropause checklist online has the same problem.

A checklist names what you're feeling. It can't name why. Tiredness, low mood and low drive are the four or five signals the body reaches for no matter what's wrong. Poor sleep produces them. Stress produces them. So does low vitamin D, an underactive thyroid, too much alcohol, or genuinely low testosterone. Same symptoms, four or five different causes, and no quiz can separate them.

So you read the list, you recognise yourself in nine of the fourteen items, and you conclude you've got the thing the list is named after. Everyone does it. It's what the format is built to make you do.

You don't have a symptom problem. You have a "which cause is it" problem. And that's the one a checklist was never built to solve.

## Do all men go through it, and at what age?

No, and this is where the female-menopause comparison falls apart.

The menopause is universal and time-bound: it happens to every woman, within a fairly predictable window. Andropause is neither. There's no set age it starts and no event every man passes through. The testosterone decline begins its slow drift from around 30 to 40, but for many men that causes no problems at all (NHS, [The 'male menopause'](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/male-menopause/)).

Some men notice a real change in their 40s or 50s. Others sail through both decades and feel fine. A genuine, more significant testosterone deficiency that develops later in life, called late-onset hypogonadism, does exist, but it's an uncommon and specific medical condition, not a normal part of ageing (NHS, [The 'male menopause'](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/male-menopause/)).

So the age on your birth certificate doesn't decide it. Two men turn fifty-two on the same day, and one of them is fine.

## What else could it be?

Most of what gets blamed on testosterone is caused by something else, and several of those causes are easier to change.

**Sleep.** Broken or short sleep flattens energy, mood and drive on its own, and it drags testosterone down as a bonus. It's the first thing to look at, not the last.

**Stress.** Stress that never switches off keeps your cortisol up and your testosterone down. If that's your last two years rather than your last two weeks, it's worth reading about in its own right: [what stress does to the male body](/blog/signs-of-stress-in-men).

**Low vitamin D.** Common in UK men from October to March, and its symptoms (tiredness, low mood, aches) overlap almost exactly with the andropause list. Worth ruling in or out: [the signs of low vitamin D](/blog/low-vitamin-d-symptoms).

**Thyroid.** An underactive thyroid mimics low testosterone, and it's easy to miss. See [what a thyroid test checks](/blog/thyroid-test).

**Plain tiredness with a hidden cause.** Iron, B12 and a handful of other markers explain a lot of everyday fatigue. If "always tired" is the headline, start with [why you might always be tired](/blog/why-am-i-always-tired).

None of this means testosterone isn't part of your picture. It might be. It means you can't know until you separate the causes, and the honest way to do that is to measure rather than assume.

## How you find out: test your testosterone

The next step isn't a supplement and it isn't a self-diagnosis. It's a number.

A testosterone blood test shows you where your level sits, so you stop guessing whether it's the thing to look at. That's all it does, and that's why it's useful. It turns "I feel off and I've been reading about andropause" into "my total testosterone is 14.2 nmol/L, taken at eight in the morning."

If your testosterone comes back mid-range and healthy, that's good news, and it points you back at the other causes above. If it's on the low side, you've got a real signal to take further. Either way you've stopped guessing.

### Total vs free testosterone, and why morning timing matters

Timing first. Testosterone runs on a daily rhythm, highest in the early morning and drifting down through the day. UK guidance is to take the sample in the morning, between 7 and 11am, and to confirm a low reading on a second morning test rather than acting on one (Hackett et al., 2023, [BSSM guidelines](https://doi.org/10.5534/wjmh.221027)). A sample drawn at 4pm can read low for no reason other than the clock. Men get told they're deficient on the strength of an afternoon appointment.

Then which testosterone you measure. Most of the testosterone in your blood is bound to proteins (SHBG and albumin) and can't be used. Only about 1 to 4% is free and active (Lab Tests Online UK, [Testosterone test](https://labtestsonline.org.uk/tests/testosterone-test)). So a "normal" total can still hide a low usable amount. If you want the full version of that argument, we've written it up in the [free androgen index](/blog/free-androgen-index) guide and in [the myth of the normal range](/blog/myth-of-normal-range). This article's job is just to get you to the number; those two explain how to read it.

## Is there a treatment for andropause?

There's no andropause treatment to buy, and be wary of anyone selling you one.

That sounds like a letdown. It's more useful than a shortcut that doesn't exist.

The first honest move isn't a product. It's finding out your actual number, because a good share of the men reading this don't have a testosterone problem at all, and a shortcut aimed at the wrong cause is money and hope down the drain.

The second is lifestyle, and it does real work here. Losing excess weight, lifting weights, sleeping properly, and easing off alcohol all move how men in this bracket feel, and several of them nudge testosterone in the right direction too. None of it is glamorous and none of it is instant. We've laid out the levers that matter in [how to increase testosterone naturally](/blog/how-to-increase-testosterone-naturally).

The third, if your testosterone is repeatedly low, is your GP. That's a medical conversation, not a wellness one, and not something you sort out from a blog. What we do is narrow: we test, we explain the number, and we tell you the next step, including when that step is your GP. We don't diagnose and we don't prescribe.

## When this is a GP conversation, not a test

Some of this doesn't belong in a wellness test at all. It belongs with your doctor first.

**Erectile problems.** Take these to your GP, not a testosterone test. Erectile difficulty can be an early warning sign of narrowed arteries, because the blood vessels involved are small and show strain first, so it can flag heart risk well before anything else does (British Heart Foundation, [Erectile dysfunction](https://www.bhf.org.uk/informationsupport/heart-matters-magazine/medical/erectile-dysfunction)). That's a doctor's assessment, not something to route through a hormone panel.

**Low mood or loss of interest.** If your mood is low, or you've lost interest in things you used to enjoy, please talk to your GP. That's a mental-health conversation and it deserves one, not a blood test and not a supplement.

**Symptoms affecting your daily life or your relationship.** If this is getting in the way of how you live, start with your GP. That's not a wellness conversation.

**You're under 40 with these symptoms.** Age-related testosterone decline is less likely to be the cause this early, which makes it more worth having a doctor look properly rather than assuming it's your hormones.

And if a test does show a low number, the same rule holds. A total testosterone that comes back below 12 nmol/L is a reason to see your GP, not to buy anything (Hackett et al., 2023, [BSSM guidelines](https://doi.org/10.5534/wjmh.221027)). There's no kit and no supplement being sold into that result. We'd rather point you to a doctor than keep you.
