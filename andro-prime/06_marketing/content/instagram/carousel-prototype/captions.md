## The one rule this file exists to protect

**One caption per topic. The same caption on all three of that topic's posts. Never varied by close.**

`slide-8-closes.md:7`: *"Slides 1 to 7 are identical across all three closes, so the close is the only
variable in the run."* A caption that changes with the close, or that echoes the close's ask, makes
itself a second variable. The 30 days then answer "which close plus caption bundle worked", which is not
a question anyone asked and cannot be untangled afterwards.

So there are **10 captions, each used 3 times**, and every one of them is **close-neutral**:

- **No price and no kit name.** That is close B's job. A caption naming £119 turns every post into a
  direct offer and deletes the distinction between B and the other two.
- **No "take the quiz" and no "three questions".** That is close A.
- **No "read the full article" and no "every source linked".** That is close C, and it is the exact
  phrasing of C's second line.
- **"Link in bio." is safe and is the one CTA used**, because all three closes already end on it. It is
  common to all arms, so it discriminates between none of them.

**This is deliberately not the only defensible design.** If the real question is "which whole post
converts" rather than "which close converts", captions *should* reinforce their close. That is a
different experiment and it is Keith's to choose. It is not what CA-031 ratified, so it is not what is
written here.

**Ruled (Keith, 2026-08-12): keep the ten close-neutral captions.** He was offered the 30 matched
alternative and a split design (neutral for days 1 to 15, matched after), and took neither. The split
was the worst of the three on the numbers: it would have left each close with 5 posts under one design
and 5 under another, halving the n on the only comparison the run buys, on top of the weakening K2
already describes.

**Known side effect, accepted: three posts per topic will carry identical caption text**, 10 days apart.
Instagram's duplicate-content heuristics are mostly about identical media, and this run already ships
near-identical media three times per topic (only slide 8 differs), so the marginal risk from the caption
is small and the alternative costs the experiment. Flagged rather than hidden.

## What the copy does

**Written as an allowlist on purpose.** Observation 53 (ACTIONED 2026-07-31, now `compliance-preflight`
step 4): a compliance note that enumerates what it avoided puts the banned terms back into the artefact
the rule governs, and a reviewer who sees ambers on every file, always from the drafter's own footnote,
stops reading ambers. That is how a real one gets waved through. So this section states what the
captions **do**, not what they avoid. Same information, scanner-clean.

- **Every fact below is already in its deck**, which was itself compressed from the published,
  Ewa-reviewed article. **No caption introduces a fact the carousel does not already carry.** This keeps
  the derivative rule intact: a caption cannot exceed its canonical.
- **No CRP band table.** The captions carry "a standard CRP of 2 reads normal, the same 2 on the hs-CRP
  framework reads average to elevated" and "over 10 is your GP that week", and **stop there**. Restating
  *"1.0 to 3.0 is average"* in a caption would reproduce **E1 on a fresh surface**, one that has no room
  for the reconciling line the slide just gained. The middle band stays on the slide, where its
  qualifier lives.
- **No threshold that the results engine would contradict.** Active B12 "under 25 is low" matches
  `classifier.ts:325` (NICE NG239, ratified by Ewa 2026-06-16 and again 2026-08-07). The one place this
  needed a human was `myth-of-normal-range`, ruled by Keith below.
- **One ingredient sentence, quoted exactly.** *"Zinc contributes to the maintenance of normal
  testosterone levels"* is the EFSA table's own string, and the next sentence is the deck's
  *"Maintenance, not boosting."* Every other reference to supplements describes the evidence base, not
  an effect. The formulation's silent ingredient appears in none of the ten.
- **The captions describe measurement and interpretation.** They say what a marker is, what a range
  answers, what makes a reading unreliable, and when to see a GP. Two of them open by telling the reader
  that what he has is a description or a set of cues rather than a medical conclusion, wording carried
  over verbatim from approved slide copy on the vitamin D and brain fog decks.
- **Attribution stays with the system, never a person.** Nothing suggests an individual result is read
  by a clinician.
- **Punctuation is colon, comma, semicolon, full stop and brackets.**
- **GP-referral language is preserved wherever the deck carries it**, and never softened: B12 nerve
  symptoms, ferritin as a GP question first, sudden-onset tiredness, neurological fog, CRP over 10.

### C2 · `b12-blood-test` — days 2, 12, 22

> Most men never find out which B12 they were actually tested for.
>
> A standard test reads total B12. Only the active fraction is the part your cells can take up and use.
> 70 to 90% of it is bound to haptocorrin, carried to the liver and excreted.
>
> So a result can read normal while the part that matters sits low.
>
> Active B12 under 25 pmol/L is low. 25 to 70 is borderline. A 40 sits inside "normal" on most reports
> and can still be low enough to matter for how you feel, especially if it has been drifting down.
>
> Reference ranges vary by lab, so read the band printed on your own report.
>
> One that is not a wait-and-see: numbness, pins and needles, or changes in balance, memory or
> concentration need a GP without delay.

### C3 · `ferritin-blood-test` — days 3, 13, 23

> Ferritin is the tank, not the fuel.
>
> It measures the iron you hold in reserve, and the tank empties first. That is why ferritin falls weeks
> or months before anyone is frankly anaemic, and why a full blood count can still read normal while
> your stores are low. That is the version most often missed.
>
> What low tends to look like in active men: flat energy a full night doesn't shift, legs heavy in the
> last third of a session, breathless on efforts that never touched you, recovery that has quietly
> slipped.
>
> Every one of those has other causes. That is exactly why you test instead of guessing.
>
> High is not automatically more iron either. Ferritin rises with inflammation, infection, recent
> alcohol and liver strain, all independent of how much iron you actually hold.
>
> A low result is a GP question first. Iron carries a real overdose risk, and in men depleted stores can
> point to slow blood loss. The cause gets found first. We don't sell iron.

### C4 · `crp-blood-test` — days 4, 14, 24

> The same CRP of 2 is normal on one report and elevated on another.
>
> A standard CRP of 2 reads normal on an NHS printout. The same 2 on the hs-CRP framework reads average
> to elevated. Different questions, one figure on the page.
>
> CRP itself only says a response is happening. Your liver releases it when your immune system is
> reacting to something. It doesn't tell you what. It tells you that it's happening, and how much.
>
> Five things that make one reading lie: a cold or flu in the last fortnight, a hard session in the last
> 48 hours, dental work in the last week, a fresh vaccination, anything acute you can name.

> under six hours, body composition, alcohol. Usually a slow accumulation, often two of them stacked.
>
> Retest four to eight weeks later, when you're not acutely ill and didn't train brutally the day
> before. That's the reading that means something.

> You're not lazy.
>
> There's a difference between sleepy and tired all the time. Sleepy you can sort tonight. The flat
> feeling is an accumulation: small inputs, stacked, over weeks.

>

> men it isn't a brain problem.

### C7 · `free-androgen-index` — days 7, 17, 27

> Your GP measured how much testosterone is in your blood. Not how much of it your body can reach.
>
> About 45% is bound tightly to SHBG. Most of the rest is loosely held by albumin. Only 1 to 3% is
> completely free. The total counts the locked-up and the free as if they were the same thing.
>
> So when SHBG is high, more of your total gets tied up while the total itself reads comfortably
> mid-range the whole time. That is the gap between "your total is normal" and "you still feel off".
>
> One correction worth having: UK labs don't report Free Androgen Index for men. They report a
> calculated free testosterone instead, and report FAI for women. If you already have an FAI on a
> report, read it as a rough signal, not as your usable number.
>
> And an honest limit: with SHBG sitting inside its reference range, a calculated free testosterone
> tells you nothing your total hasn't already told you. These figures earn their keep when SHBG is high
> or low, not as a routine upgrade on every test.
>
> Link in bio.
>
> Education, not medical advice.

### C8 · `how-to-increase-testosterone-naturally` — days 8, 18, 28

> The real list is boring and free.
>
> Body fat. Strength training. Sleep. Alcohol. Stress. Roughly that order of impact for most men, and
> the rest is noise.
>
> Why body fat first: fat tissue carries an enzyme called aromatase, which converts testosterone into
> oestrogen. A review pooling 24 studies found weight loss, by diet or surgery, was followed by a
> meaningful rise, and the more men lost, the bigger the rise.

> The overlooked one is sleep. Ten healthy young men slept under five hours a night for a week. Their

> maintenance of normal testosterone levels. Read that carefully. Maintenance, not boosting.
>
> There is no overnight switch. The levers move over weeks to months. Build the habits, give them eight
> to twelve weeks, then measure, so you can see what actually moved instead of guessing.

### C9 · `myth-of-normal-range` — days 9, 19, 29

> A man at 9 nmol/L and a man at 25 nmol/L are handed the same sentence: within normal range.
>
> A typical UK lab band runs roughly 8 to 29 nmol/L, and it shifts from lab to lab. It was built to flag
> clinical illness, not to describe wellness.
>
> What went into that band: age, which takes testosterone down roughly 1 to 2% a year from 30.
> 18-year-olds and 70-year-olds in one pot. A population that has grown heavier. Athletes and sedentary
> men in the same sample. Normal today is lower than normal was thirty years ago.
>
> In 3,369 European men aged 40 to 79, three or more classic symptoms became significantly more likely
> below 11 nmol/L.
>
> The framework isn't lying. It's answering whether you are ill. Most men reading their result are
> asking whether they are well. A baseline you can retest tells you more than where you fall on one wide
> band.
>
> Link in bio.

> Education, not medical advice.

### C10 · `how-to-read-blood-test-results` — days 10, 20, 30

> Every line on a blood test says four things. Marker, value, units, reference range.
>
> See those four on any line and you can read any panel.
>
> The reference range is the part people misread. The lab tests a large group it considers healthy and
> draws the band around about 95% of them. By definition that leaves 1 in 20 perfectly healthy people
> outside it.
>
> Units matter as much as the number. nmol/L for testosterone and SHBG. mg/L for CRP. µg/L for ferritin.
> pmol/L for active B12. g/L for haemoglobin. A number means nothing without its unit.
>
> Timing matters too. Testosterone runs on a daily rhythm and is highest in the early morning, so a 4pm
> figure read against a morning-calibrated range can look low because of the clock.
>
> And one number outside the band is not a label. A cold, a brutal session, a poor night, dehydration,
> the meal before the test. Any of them nudges a marker without your baseline having moved at all.
>
> One reading tells you where you are today. It can't tell you which way you're moving.
>
> Link in bio.
>
> Education, not medical advice.
