# Kallaway: three frameworks

Tactical extraction of three transcripts. Recorded here because the earlier synthesis into `hook-playbook.md` and `script-playbook.md` lost material nobody could audit (see `README.md`).

> **Read the compliance section at the bottom before using any of this.** One of the three frameworks contains a rule that Andro Prime **must not** adopt. It is not a close call.

---

## 1. The illusion of novelty (making a boring category interesting)

His premise: addictive content is **relevant, novel and interesting**. Boring categories can nail relevant and struggle with the other two. Five steps to manufacture the missing pair.

**Step 1. New reveal + outcome mapping.** Reveal that something about an existing thing has changed, then tie it to an outcome the viewer wants. The reveal makes them look; the outcome makes them stay. Crucially, the reveal does **not** need a new topic. An old topic with a new angle, frame, name, or finding works the same way.

**Step 2. Contrast framing.** Position the new reveal directly against what the audience already believes. "New" means nothing except relative to "old". The tactical rule: the contrast must be a **true opposite** of the original belief, held constant. An unrelated old thing creates confusion, not contrast. Shapes he gives: *Everyone thinks X, but actually it's Y. You've been told X your whole life, turns out X is wrong. X used to be the gold standard, but Y makes X obsolete.*

**Step 3. Urgency (the turbo button).** Optional accelerator, only when a real time window exists. He is explicit: **never bolt on fake urgency.** "If people smell this fake urgency, immediately you lose all trust and it feels like you're trying to sell."

**Step 4. Bullseye proof (the trust ladder).** Evidence for the reveal, ranked by how close it sits to the viewer's own life. Top rung wins because of self-identification: people distrust third-party claims and trust versions of themselves getting the result.

| Rung | Proof | Strength |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | A single example that mimics the viewer: same age, same problem, same situation. If the creator is the same demographic, a result the creator experienced counts. | Strongest |
| 2 | The warm crowd. Generalised "a lot of people got this result". | Middle |
| 3 | Third-party verification: a study, an article, a stat, an award. | Weakest, still works |

Climb as high as the truth allows. His own caveat: "if the proof isn't there you don't manufacture a lie."

**Step 5. Protect the illusion.** Two halves.

- *Don't show the mascot.* Never reveal that the new frame is an old thing dressed up. Commit to the line, don't hedge or downplay. **See the compliance section. Andro Prime rejects this half.**
- *Gossip whisperer, not town crier.* Delivery, not content. "Huge news, this changes everything" reads as a billboard and triggers sales resistance. Lowering your voice as though letting someone in on something reads as sharing, not selling.

## 2. The addiction loop (how to hold attention after the hook)

**This is the piece `script-playbook.md` never got.** Its four blockers name "Story: how do I hold them after the hook?" as the third blocker and then answer it thinly. This is the tactical answer.

The premise is a correction: **dopamine is the prediction chemical, not the pleasure chemical.** It fires while anticipating an outcome, not on receiving it. A vending machine is not addictive because the result is certain. A slot machine is, because it isn't. Uncertainty is the whole mechanism.

**Step 1. Stakes.** Turns the prediction machine on. Without a reason to care the brain predicts nothing, and no prediction means no dopamine. Needs exactly three things:

1. A character to root for. Can be you, a client, "a bloke I was talking to". Just a real human in a real situation.
2. Something at risk, so the brain knows what failure looks like.
3. Urgency. "Within a week" beats "eventually". "The call was in two hours" beats "later that day".

Stakes do **not** mean life or death. They mean personally relevant to the intended viewer.

**Step 2. The big question.** Load a question the brain wants to resolve. The common failure is vagueness: "something crazy happened", "you won't believe it". Those are empty teasers, because the viewer cannot form a prediction from them, and with no prediction there is nothing for the reveal to contrast against. Give **enough information to predict, not so much as to overwhelm.** Load it early: the longer it sits unresolved, the longer the dopamine drips.

**Step 3. The head fake.** Break the prediction. Neurologically this is *prediction error*, and it produces the largest dopamine spike available; the size of the spike scales with the size of the surprise. The hard part is that a surprise can be addictive or merely confusing:

- **A cheap surprise** comes from nowhere, unconnected to the setup, and produces frustration.
- **A great head fake** is one the viewer would never have predicted but immediately understands as true once they hear it, because the clues were there.

His method: work out what a normal viewer *should* be predicting from what you have already said, then contrast against precisely that. The magician analogy: show the hat, show it's empty, direct attention one way, produce the rabbit the other.

**Step 4. The rehook.** Close one loop and open the next **in the same breath**, before the viewer finishes processing. The casino metric he cites is time-per-hand: the entire goal is shrinking the gap between dopamine hits. There should never be a moment where the viewer thinks "right, I'm done".

The danger zones are exactly the points where you finish a section. Connective phrases that close and open simultaneously:

> which would have been great, except… · but here's the problem with that… · and that's when I realised… · which is exactly why…

His relay-race image: the baton passes while both runners are moving. A dead stop loses all momentum.

## 3. The hook machine (a data workflow, not a craft rule)

An AI workflow rather than a writing framework. Requires a Sandcastles account plus its MCP connected to Claude, so it is **not currently actionable for Andro Prime** and is recorded for completeness. The transferable ideas:

- **Why generic AI hooks fail:** LLMs are not trained on short-form social performance data, so a cold prompt produces generic output.
- **Why copy-pasting a viral hook across niches fails:** the psychology doesn't transfer, and neither does the grammar or rhythm. Format cross-pollination breaks. His own tool refuses to force a case-study hook template onto a conceptual topic, which he treats as correct behaviour.
- **Screen out paid posts before learning from them.** Any high performer under about 2% engagement probably had spend behind it and will teach you the wrong lesson.
- **Find the natural break point** in views to separate winners from losers, rather than a fixed top-N.
- **Analyse one competitor at a time.** Pooling several dilutes the findings toward the average; separate passes stay sharp.
- **Grade your own draft against the rubric and ask for rewrites.** He rates this the most useful part of the whole workflow.
- Analysis dimensions he uses: psychology, trigger words and framing, grammatical structure, non-obvious patterns.

Two findings from his own data worth noting, because they **independently confirm rules Andro Prime already holds**: winners avoid throat-clearing openers, and confident declaratives beat hedged questions.

That last row is the interesting one. Keith's unscripted Achilles story runs a textbook head fake: several hundred words of ordinary dojo routine build a confident prediction, then "pop" breaks it, and the clues were there in advance. **He already does this naturally.** The framework names it and makes it repeatable.

### The genuinely new material

The **addiction loop** is the gap. `script-playbook.md` names "Story" as its third blocker and answers it with four story structures. Stakes → big question → head fake → rehook is the mechanism underneath those structures, and the rehook in particular has no equivalent anywhere in the repo.

The **trust ladder** is the other one, and it is uncomfortable in a useful way. Andro Prime's proof is almost entirely rung 3, the weakest: NHS ranges, Wu et al., BSSM guidelines. Rung 1 requires a person who mimics the viewer. Keith is the right demographic, which is why founder content works at all, but his own history is not a clean mimic of the target customer's, so his results cannot carry the mechanism even where they carry the feeling. Rung 2 becomes available as customer results accumulate. **Worth an explicit decision rather than drift.**

### The rule Andro Prime must reject

**"Don't show the mascot" is incompatible with this business.** Three reasons, and they compound:

1. **It contradicts the positioning.** The 2026-07-22 conflict-free decision is built on separation of incentives and "we'd rather lose a sale than have a man read a wellness blog when he needs to be sitting in front of his GP". A brand whose entire differentiator is not manipulating you cannot adopt a rule about concealing that its framing is manufactured.
2. **It is a regulatory problem, not just a brand one.** In health advertising, presenting long-established science as a new discovery, and deliberately withholding that it is old, is misleading by omission. That is ASA/CAP territory regardless of intent.
3. **Andro Prime does not need it.** This is the important part. The brand's central claim is a *genuine* new reveal with a *genuine* contrast frame: the NHS reference range was built to flag illness, not to define wellness, so "within range" does not mean "well". It is true, it is cited, and it is genuinely counter to what the viewer walked in believing. There is no illusion to protect because the novelty is real.

**Adopt steps 1, 2, 3 and 4. Adopt the gossip-whisperer half of step 5. Reject the concealment half outright.**

Where a topic genuinely has no honest new angle, the answer is the one `hook-playbook.md` §1 already gives: do not make the video. There is always another idea.
