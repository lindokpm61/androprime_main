# X week 1: the normal range

**Source:** `myth-of-normal-range`, published 2026-06-19, Ewa-signed. Every claim below is already in that article. Nothing net-new, so this batch inherits its sign-off.

**Rails, written as what the copy does** (an allowlist, per the CONTEXT.md rule that a note listing prohibited terms re-triggers the scanner on itself):

- Describes what a reference range is and where it comes from. Makes no statement about any individual reader's result.
- Every figure is quoted from the canonical article: the 8 to 29 nmol/L range, the 260% span, the 1 to 2% annual decline from 30, the 15 to 30% loss by 45 and over 40% by 60, UK obesity nearly tripling since 1980, the 2.5th and 97.5th percentile cut. Verified line by line against the article 2026-07-31, including the aromatase and estradiol mechanism in thread post 5.
- States the fatigue association exactly as the article does, as a statistical pattern in the 8 to 12 band, and leaves it there.
- Routes nowhere except the article itself. No product, no kit, no list.
- Founder post restates an already-approved surface verbatim. See the scope note below.
- Punctuation stays within the brand rule: colons, commas, semicolons, full stops, brackets.

**Range wording corrected 2026-07-31.** Monday, Thursday and thread post 2 originally asserted "the NHS testosterone range" as a single national fact. **There is no single NHS reference range:** each laboratory derives its own from its analyser and reference population, which is why `04_products/results-engine/thresholds.md` records NHS trusts disagreeing with each other on SHBG and albumin, and why Keith's own November 2025 report printed **6 to 22 nmol/L** rather than 8 to 29. Left as written, this batch would have contradicted Keith's own results on camera in the same week (`assets/2026-07-31-same-test-twice.md` quotes his sheet), and Monday's "one at 9, one at 25, both normal" illustration does not hold on a 6 to 22 range, where 25 sits above it. All three now say "a typical NHS range" or "an NHS blood report" rather than "the NHS range".

**Not added, deliberately.** The stronger point, that reference ranges vary between labs, is **not in the canonical article** (which itself says "the NHS uses one band"), so putting it in this batch would be a net-new claim needing Ewa. The batch is corrected to stop overclaiming; it does not gain a new claim. The article's own imprecision is logged separately.

## Sunday, thread

**Post by hand.** Metricool will not split X copy into a thread, so this one is manual until that is tested.

**1/**
> Why "your testosterone is normal" might not mean what you think.
>
> A thread on where that range actually comes from.

**2/**
> A typical NHS range runs roughly 8 to 29 nmol/L.
>
> It is not a healthy band. It is a statistical one: the spread you get from sampling adult men aged 18 to 70, then cutting at the 2.5th and 97.5th percentile.

**3/**
> Who is in that sample?
>
> Athletes. Sedentary men. Lean and overweight. 22-year-olds and 68-year-olds.
>
> All in one pot.

**4/**
> Age does most of the work.
>
> Testosterone peaks in the early 20s and falls roughly 1 to 2% a year from 30.
>
> By 45 the average man has lost 15 to 30% of his peak. By 60, more than 40%.
>
> That decline is baked into the range.

**5/**
> The population moved as well.
>
> Body fat contains aromatase, which converts testosterone into estradiol. UK obesity has nearly tripled since 1980.
>
> The range gets resampled over time. Normal today is lower than normal was thirty years ago.

**6/**
> So the range is wide because it was built to fit men decades apart in age and in health.
>
> Not because it describes a well man.

**7/**
> None of this means the framework is broken. It answers the question it was built for.
>
> It is just not the question you are asking when you feel worse than you did five years ago.
>
> Full article: https://andro-prime.com/blog/myth-of-normal-range
