> He had booked the gut test before he had mentioned the takeover.
>
> Stomach in knots for a month. Two people gone from his team, a takeover going through at work, and he wasn't sleeping. None of it came up until I asked.
>
> Not what had changed in his diet. What had changed in his life.
>
> Then he listed it. The listing was the whole conversation.
>
> He didn't have a stomach problem. He had a stress load his gut was keeping score of.
>
> When your brain reads a threat it floods you with adrenaline and cortisol. Heart rate up, muscles tense, senses sharp. For a genuine short-term threat that's a brilliant piece of engineering. The trouble is your brain runs the identical programme for a deadline, a mortgage or a difficult boss, and it doesn't switch off at six in the evening.
>
> One thing interrupts the rest of this, because the order matters. Sudden or severe chest pain, pain that spreads to your arm, neck, jaw or back, or chest pain with breathlessness, sweating or feeling sick, means call 999 now. Don't talk yourself out of it. Don't assume it's stress. Rule out your heart first, every time.
>
> Cortisol does one more thing worth knowing. It quietly stands down whatever the body decides isn't urgent in a crisis: digestion, sleep, skin repair, sex drive. Useful for an afternoon. Not for a year.
>
> So the gut gets a test, the sleep gets a tracker, the headaches get paracetamol. Three errands, three receipts, and the thing underneath all three never gets named once.
>
> I'm building a company that sells blood tests, and what he needed that week wasn't a test.
>
> Stress doesn't show up cleanly on a single blood test. We don't measure cortisol, so there's no stress check to sell you here. What a long stretch of running hot does reach is the things you can measure over time: your energy, your sleep, the way your body recovers. That's what a baseline is for. Different conversation, different week.
>
> The line that doesn't bend: stress that's lasted weeks and is affecting your sleep, work or relationships is a GP conversation. Same if it comes with a low mood that won't lift, or you've stopped enjoying things you used to. Same if you're leaning harder on alcohol to cope, or self-help isn't shifting it. And same if a physical symptom, gut, chest, skin or headaches, won't settle once the pressure's eased.
>
> For the mental-health side, you don't have to start with us. The NHS Every Mind Matters service and Mind both have direct, free support. Stress that's taken over isn't a weakness to manage alone. We're not a mental health service, and a blood test doesn't quieten a life that's too loud.
>
> What he got instead was smaller than a test. Pick two or three things, hold them for a fortnight, see what lifts.
>
> What did you book, or buy, before anyone asked you what had actually changed that month?

## Rails held

Written as an allowlist of what the copy does, never a list of what it avoids, because a note that spells out the regulated vocabulary puts it back into the file the scanner then reads (Observation 83).

The post stays on the education side of the line throughout. It describes a mechanism, attributes it to the article's cited sources, and offers no outcome. It carries the emergency instruction for sudden or severe chest pain in the paragraph immediately after the one that names a raised heart rate, word for word as the signed article gives it. It names the GP as the route on all four of the article's triggers: stress lasting weeks, a low mood that won't lift or lost enjoyment, leaning harder on alcohol or self-help not shifting it, and a physical symptom that won't settle once the pressure has eased. It then names NHS Every Mind Matters and Mind as places a reader may start instead of with us, before stating what the business is not, so the boundary follows a route rather than replacing one. It is explicit that no product is being offered for this topic and that cortisol is not something we measure, which keeps it inside the content-only rule for markers with no kit. It names no ingredient and asserts no benefit, so the EFSA tables are not in play. Its founder reveal cuts against the commercial interest rather than toward it. The single CTA is a soft link in the first comment routed to the canonical article, carrying the full `https://` scheme and a UTM. Sentence-level: contractions throughout, no em dashes, no bullet lists in the post body, one link, no engagement bait.
