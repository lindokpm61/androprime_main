Going out next

every scheduled post, individually

One row per scheduled rendition, not one per channel. The coverage grid below collapses every post against an article into a single cell, so a week of posts and a single post look identical there. This is the view that tells them apart. Times are Europe/London. A day carrying more than one post is marked, because two posts running the same idea hours apart reads as a tic.

What needs you

computed, not remembered

Derived from the database's own gates each time this loads. An asset cannot be approved without a green pre-flight, and a rendition cannot publish while the article it inherits its claims from is unpublished. Nothing here is a stored to-do list; if a row disappears it is because the condition cleared.

What each article has become

the atomisation grid

One row per published article, one column per planned channel. A cell counts the renditions behind it, so seven posts against one article read as seven and not as one. Empty cells are the backlog: an article that could carry that channel and does not. Slugs are printed under each title because articles get retitled and the slug is what everything else keys on.

What is live

newest first

Published and measured renditions only; anything still scheduled is in "Going out next" above rather than missing. Metrics are captured into the database rather than read from the platforms, so this is only as current as the last capture. Substack's public feed exposes no impression count, so those cells read not available rather than zero. LinkedIn counts our own first comment, which is subtracted here.

The pipeline

every asset, by status

Assets whose status is outside the known set would be invisible in these columns, so any that appear are listed explicitly at the end rather than silently dropped.

Channels

wired against planned

A channel that is connected but not in the plan is a channel that arrived without a lane. A channel in the plan but not connected cannot publish, whatever the queue says.

Hooks

including the rejected ones

Every hook generated is stored with its rubric score, whether it was used or not. Winners-only data cannot tell you whether the rubric scores correctly; the rejected ones are the control group.
