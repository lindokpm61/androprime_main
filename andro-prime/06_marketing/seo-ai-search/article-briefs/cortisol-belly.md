## 2. The article's job (one sentence)

Tell a man whose midsection will not shift what stress genuinely does to body fat, what the phrase
"cortisol belly" is and is not, what to change this week, and where the honest line sits between
everyday stress and something a GP should see — without diagnosing him, selling him a test that does
not exist, or naming a supplement we are barred from discussing.

## 3. Target reader (ICP)

Men 35-55. Training and eating roughly as well as they ever have, and still carrying weight around
the middle. High work load, poor sleep, drinking more than they mean to. They have seen "cortisol
belly" on TikTok or in a Boots article and want to know whether it is real or marketing. Sceptical,
mildly embarrassed, tired of being sold to. Not looking for a diagnosis, looking for a straight answer.

## 4. Search-intent decoded

Informational with a strong "is this real?" undertone. They want, in this order: (a) validation that
the pattern they have noticed is a real thing and not vanity, (b) a plain mechanism, (c) an honest
statement of how much of it is actually cortisol versus sleep, alcohol, calories and age, (d) what to
do this week, (e) when it is something else. Google's own People Also Ask confirms it:
*"How do I get rid of my cortisol belly?"*, *"How to reduce cortisol NHS?"*,
*"How long does it take for cortisol levels to return to normal?"*

Not transactional. No product pitch survives contact with this reader.

## 7. Opening block (the AI-snippet target)

2-3 sentences answering "what is cortisol belly" head-on, and **leading with the honesty**, because

"Cortisol belly" is not a medical diagnosis. It is a popular name for weight that settles around the

fat toward the abdomen, but it is one factor among several, and the amount people attribute to it is
usually larger than the evidence supports.

## 9. Section-by-section content brief

- **What it actually means.** Lead with "not a diagnosis". Give the reader the plain version: a
  popular term, not a clinical entity. This is the `myth-of-normal-range` posture the brand already
  owns, and it is also exactly what Google's AI Overview says, which helps the citation odds.
- **The mechanism.** Cortisol, the stress response, and why abdominal fat is more responsive than
  subcutaneous fat. Physiological and general. **No thresholds, no "you have high cortisol", no
  numbers attached to the reader.**
- **The part that is not cortisol (load-bearing).** The honest accounting: sleep debt, alcohol, a
  slow calorie surplus, less muscle than a decade ago. Say plainly that stress is a real contributor
  and a convenient scapegoat. **This is the section no page-one competitor writes**, and it is the
  reason a sceptical man finishes the article.
- **Why it looks different on men (the wedge).** Male fat distribution, the beer-belly pattern,
  and why the advice aimed at women in the perimenopause framing does not map cleanly. Careful,
  non-mocking. This is the section that earns `cortisol belly men`.
- **What to change this week.** 4-6 concrete behavioural levers: a fixed sleep window, an alcohol
  trim, walking, resistance training over punishing HIIT, protein and fibre at meals, and cutting
  late caffeine. **Behaviour only. No supplement efficacy of any kind. No adaptogens. Omit the whole
  category rather than saying "supplements don't work", which invites a writer to name one.**
- **Can you test for it? (the honest no).** Straight: there is no test for "cortisol belly", we do
  not sell a cortisol test, and the clinically preferred cortisol measurement is a four-sample saliva
  test that does not fit a postal finger-prick kit. Say what a check *can* show and route to the list.
  **Never imply a cortisol test is buyable from us.**
- **When it is not just stress (boundary, Ewa's gate).** GP if the weight gain is rapid and
  unexplained, or comes with the specific pattern that points at Cushing's syndrome. Name Cushing's
  once, calmly, as the rare thing to rule out, and signpost NHS. **Do not build a symptom checklist**
  a reader can self-diagnose from. Ewa sight-checks this section's wording.

   It is a writer adding it to be helpful, or writing a "supplements that don't work" section that
   names it in order to dismiss it.** The v2.2 partner-brief breach was exactly that shape: the
   ingredient printed inside its own prohibited-list. **Omit the entire supplement category.** A

   general fatigue or body-composition symptoms, which is this exact cohort. **No low-T framing, no
   FM CTA** (FM only on confirmed T < 12 nmol/L, never inferred).
4. **Cushing's boundary, Ewa's gate.** Name it once, calmly, signpost NHS. Do **not** build a
   self-diagnosis checklist. The `high cortisol symptoms` SERP is a rare-disease SERP and we are

5. **Weight and body-image register.** Men 35-55 being told about their midsection. No shaming, no

6. **Standing HARD rules.** No `diagnose` / `treat` / `cure`. No biomarker thresholds. No `fix` in a
