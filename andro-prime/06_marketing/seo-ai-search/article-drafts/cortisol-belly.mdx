"Cortisol belly" isn't a medical term. It's a popular name for weight that settles around the middle when pressure, broken sleep and heavy weeks have been running for months. A long stress load does push fat toward the abdomen. It's one of four things pulling the same way, and it gets blamed for more than the evidence behind it supports.

## What "cortisol belly" actually means

The suit came out of the wardrobe on the Thursday for a wedding on the Saturday. Same suit as two other weddings. Same one that went to a funeral the year before last.

The trousers didn't do up.

Nothing in his week explained it. Gym three mornings, same as the last four years. Eating roughly what he'd always eaten. Same job, same house, same route to work.

Except it hadn't been the same. Not for about eighteen months.

So the question wasn't what he'd been eating. It was what kind of year he'd had.

A restructure that took nine months to land. His dad in and out of hospital through the spring. A second child who still wasn't sleeping through, so neither was he.

He didn't have a discipline problem. He had eighteen months his body had been keeping score of.

That pattern has a name now. Cortisol belly, or stress belly, picked up from wellness posts and short videos and since repeated by pharmacy chains and health sites.

No doctor uses the phrase. It won't appear on a referral letter or a set of results. It's a popular name for something men notice, not a condition anyone gets told they have.

That doesn't make it nonsense. It makes it shorthand, and some of what's underneath is real.

## What a stress load genuinely does to where fat sits

When your brain reads a threat, it releases cortisol. Cortisol is the hormone that keeps you upright in a crisis: it frees up sugar for fuel, sharpens attention, and quietly parks the jobs your body decides can wait.

For an afternoon, that's useful.

For a year and a half, it's a different proposition.

### Why the middle, specifically

Not all fat behaves the same way. The fat under your skin, the bit you can pinch, is fairly inert. The fat packed deeper, around the organs behind your abdominal wall, is not. That deeper store has a name you'll see everywhere: visceral fat.

Visceral fat carries far more cortisol receptors than the fat under your skin, and it makes more of its own active cortisol through an enzyme called 11-beta-HSD1, which converts the inactive form into the active one inside the fat cell itself. More receptors and local production together mean the deep abdominal store responds harder to a long stress load than anywhere else on you (Lee et al., 2014, [Deconstructing the roles of glucocorticoids in adipose tissue biology and the development of central obesity](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23735216/)).

That's the mechanism, and it's genuine. It's also less settled than the confident version you'll read elsewhere. The same review says plainly that the molecular details of how this works differently in different fat stores "are only beginning to be understood."

Which is the honest state of it. A real mechanism, incompletely mapped.

### The part that isn't cortisol

This is the section nobody selling you something will write.

The study everyone points to when they talk about stress and belly fat is Epel and colleagues, published in *Psychosomatic Medicine* in 2000. Women with more central fat produced more cortisol under laboratory stress, and kept producing it on day two and day three when the lean ones had adapted (Epel et al., 2000, [Stress and body shape: stress-induced cortisol secretion is consistently greater among women with central fat](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11020091/)).

Two things about that study rarely travel with the headline.

It looked at 59 people. All of them women, all premenopausal. Not one man.

And it was cross-sectional, meaning it photographed a moment rather than following anyone forward. The authors were careful about this in their own words: the findings "support the hypothesis that stress-induced cortisol secretion **may** contribute to central fat." May contribute. That's the claim, and it's a reasonable one. It is not the same claim as "stress caused your belly."

Now put the rest of the year next to it.

Sleep. Adults need seven to nine hours (NHS, [Insomnia](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/insomnia/)). A man running on four for most of a year isn't in a cortisol story, he's in a sleep-debt story that happens to raise cortisol as one of its effects.

Drink. A pint of five per cent beer carries up to 222 calories, and alcohol itself has seven calories per gram, nearly as many as fat. The NHS puts it bluntly: regularly drinking above the guidelines "can have a noticeable impact on your waistline" (NHS, [Calories in alcohol](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/alcohol-advice/calories-in-alcohol/)). Four pints on a Friday and a bottle of red across the weekend is most of a day's food that nobody counts.

Muscle. From about 30, muscle mass falls roughly three to eight per cent per decade, and faster after 60 (Volpi et al., 2004, [Muscle tissue changes with aging](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2804956/)). A man of 45 carrying less muscle than he did at 30 is burning less at rest for exactly the same day.

Sleep debt. Drinking weeks. Muscle he stopped building. None of them feel like a cause, which is precisely why cortisol gets the blame.

It's not that stress isn't doing anything. It's that stress is the one explanation on that list that doesn't ask anything awkward of you.

## Cortisol belly in men: why it looks different

Search the term and most of what comes back is written for women. The research skews that way, as the study above shows, and so does the advice: much of it is framed around perimenopause, which is a real thing and not this thing.

Men carry fat centrally by default, and more so with age. That makes the signal harder to read on us, not easier. A woman who starts storing centrally has changed pattern. A man who starts storing centrally has done more of what he was already doing, so there's no line where it began.

You'll see "cortisol face" alongside it, meaning facial puffiness. Same caveat, and even less behind it.

None of that means men are exempt. It means the reference points online were built on someone else's body, and if you've read three articles and none of them sounded like your situation, that's why.

## What cortisol belly actually looks like, and what it doesn't

Gradual. Months into years, not weeks. A belt notch, then another one a year later.

Firmer than you'd expect, often. Deep abdominal fat sits behind the muscle wall rather than on top of it, so a midsection can feel solid rather than soft.

And it tracks the hard stretch. Ask most men to line up the last two years and they'll point straight at it. The restructure. The bereavement. The year the sleep went.

What it doesn't look like is fast. Weight that arrives over weeks, with nothing you can point to, isn't this.

## What to change this week

Five things, and not one of them is exciting.

**Set the wake time before the bedtime.** The NHS advice is to get up at the same time every day, including weekends, and to keep alcohol, tea and coffee out of the six hours before bed (NHS, [Insomnia](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/insomnia/)). A consistent wake time drags the rest into place more reliably than forcing an earlier night.

**Count the drinking nights, not the drinks.** The guideline is no more than 14 units a week for men, spread across three days or more. Most men who cut two nights find the first visible change comes from there.

**Walk, most days.** The target is 150 minutes of moderate activity a week (NHS, [Physical activity guidelines for adults aged 19 to 64](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/exercise/physical-activity-guidelines-for-adults-aged-19-to-64/)). Walking counts, and it doesn't add to the load the way a punishing 6am session can.

**Lift twice a week.** Strengthening work covering the major muscle groups on at least two days, per the same guidance. This one's aimed at the muscle you've been quietly losing since 30, and it's the one most men skip.

**Eat enough protein early enough.** Not a diet. Just enough at breakfast and lunch that you're not arriving at nine at night ready to eat whatever's nearest.

There's a whole category of product marketed at this exact search, and you'll notice none of it is in that list.

That's deliberate.

## Can you test for it?

No, and I'd rather say so than dress it up.

There's no test for "cortisol belly", because it isn't a thing that gets measured. And cortisol itself is awkward to test: it rises and falls across the day by design, high in the morning, low at night, so one reading tells you almost nothing. The measurement clinicians trust takes four saliva samples across a single day.

That doesn't fit a postal finger-prick kit, which is why we don't sell a cortisol check. We've looked at it. Until it can be done properly, we'd rather not do it at all.

What a check can do is give you a baseline on the things a long stress load touches over time, so you're watching numbers move rather than guessing from how your suit fits.

## When it's not just stress

<SystemAlert title="When to book an appointment, not a gym plan" footer="Weight that arrives fast, with no reason you can name, is a GP conversation.">
  **See your GP if:**

  - The weight around your middle has come on quickly, over weeks or a few months, and you can't account for it with anything that changed.
  - You take, or have recently taken, steroid medicine.
  - Other things about your body have changed at the same time and you don't know why.

  Rarely, weight gain centred on the torso is caused by a condition called Cushing's syndrome, where the body is exposed to too much cortisol over a long period. It's uncommon, more common in women than men, and the kind of thing a GP rules out rather than something to read yourself into. The NHS page on [Cushing's syndrome](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/cushings-syndrome/) is the place to look, and its own advice is to see a GP, particularly if you're on steroid medicine.
</SystemAlert>

We're built for men who want to understand what their body's doing. We don't diagnose anything, and we're not the right first stop for a change that's happened fast. When the signal says GP, the answer is GP.

## Your next move

The man with the suit didn't change much. He moved his wake time, cut two drinking nights, and started lifting on the mornings he'd been running.

The trousers took until the following spring.

What struck me was what he said about the eighteen months. He'd registered every part of it as it happened. The restructure, his dad, the sleep. He just hadn't put them in the same sentence, because each one arrived as its own problem with its own worry attached.

I wonder how many men are sitting in the middle of a year like that right now, reading about cortisol, and not counting the year.
