its cited passages. The Overview has two content blocks. The first is **Common Lifestyle Causes**,
which our article already covers well, close to item for item. The second is **Common Medical

## The compliance reasoning, offered for you to accept or reject

**The claim being made is about a symptom in general, not about the reader.** *"Iron deficiency
anaemia causes tiredness"* is a statement of fact about a condition. *"Your tiredness is iron
deficiency anaemia"* would be a diagnosis, and nothing in the draft comes near it. The draft names
conditions, gives the mechanism, and routes every one of them to a GP.

**Three deliberate constructions, each there for a reason:**

1. **An explicit anti-self-diagnosis sentence opens the block**, before any condition is named:
   *"Knowing their names is not the same as having one."*
2. **Every bullet is attributed to the NHS**, in line with the article's existing citation style. No
   claim in the block is ours.
3. **The block ends by naming what we cannot see.** Two of the four are not measurable by anything we
   sell, and the draft says so plainly rather than leaving the impression that a kit covers it. This
   also keeps the section inside the **Kit 1 scope rule** (fatigue routes to Kit 2, never Kit 1) and
   inside the conflict-free position under CA-026.

**Placement:** immediately after your `ClinicalInsight` quote and **before** the existing
`SystemAlert` GP-referral box, so a reader who recognises themselves in the list meets the "see your
GP" instruction in the very next block. The order is doing compliance work; please keep it if you
approve.

## THE PROPOSED COPY — this is what needs your ruling

Insert after the `ClinicalInsight` block and before the `SystemAlert`, inside
`## When tiredness is more than lifestyle`.

```mdx
### The medical causes that come up most

When tiredness turns out not to be lifestyle, four things account for most of it. All four are
ordinary, all four are checkable, and none of them can be confirmed from how you feel. Knowing their
names is not the same as having one.

- **Iron deficiency anaemia.** Low iron means your blood carries less oxygen to your muscles and
  brain, so ordinary effort costs more than it should. It is the most common type of anaemia, and
  tiredness and shortness of breath are usually the first things people notice (NHS,
  [Iron deficiency anaemia](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/iron-deficiency-anaemia/)).
- **An underactive thyroid.** Your thyroid sets the pace of your metabolism, and when it runs slow
  everything slows with it. The tell is company rather than severity: tiredness alongside putting on
  weight, feeling the cold more than usual, dry skin or low mood (NHS,
  [Underactive thyroid](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/underactive-thyroid-hypothyroidism/)).
- **Low vitamin B12, or low vitamin D.** B12 deficiency causes extreme tiredness and can affect
  concentration and memory (NHS,
  [Vitamin B12 or folate deficiency anaemia](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamin-b12-or-folate-deficiency-anaemia/)).
  Low vitamin D is close to a UK default in winter: between October and early March we do not make
  enough from sunlight, and government advice is that everyone should consider a daily supplement
  through the autumn and winter (NHS,
  [Vitamin D](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/vitamins-and-minerals/vitamin-d/)).
- **Sleep apnoea.** Your breathing stops and starts through the night, so eight hours in bed does not
  buy eight hours of rest. Loud snoring, gasping or choking noises and waking up unrefreshed are the
  usual clues, and a partner often notices before you do (NHS,
  [Sleep apnoea](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/sleep-apnoea/)).

<Caveat>

**Two of those four cannot be seen in any blood test we sell, and we would rather say so than let you
assume otherwise.** The Energy & Recovery Check measures vitamin D and Active B12 directly, and
ferritin, which shows your iron stores and is the earliest part of the iron picture. It does not
confirm anaemia: that needs a full blood count, which your GP can arrange. It does not look at your
thyroid at all, and no blood test detects sleep apnoea. If one of the four above sounds like you, your
GP is the right first stop, not us.

</Caveat>
```
