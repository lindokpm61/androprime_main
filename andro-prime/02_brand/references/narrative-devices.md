# Narrative devices

`tone-of-voice.md` §2 defines five structural moves derived from five **written** samples. This file adds nine devices observed in ~11,400 words of **unscripted speech**, where Keith is building narrative in real time with nothing pre-planned.

The distinction matters. The written samples show him presenting a finished thought. The spoken corpus shows him constructing one, and construction is the thing that reads as human. An article assembled from finished thoughts reads like a brochure no matter how good the sentences are.

Use these when drafting or rewriting any article body. They are devices, not rules: reach for the one that fits the passage, don't run the list.

**This file has two layers.** Devices 1 to 9 work at the paragraph and passage level: they are what you reach for while writing a section. "The structural audit" (below, added v1.1) works at the level of the whole piece and of the piece's relationship to the last three published in the same channel. Drafting uses the devices; the voice pass runs the audit.

---

## 1. Ordinary build, then rupture

The most consistent shape in the corpus, and the one `tone-of-voice.md` has no name for.

Describe something completely mundane in patient, unhurried detail. Establish the routine, the setting, the ordinariness. Then break it in a handful of words, flat, with no dramatic escalation.

> *[the full dojo routine: arriving early, changing, stretching, katas, the last twenty minutes of sparring, who you pick as an opponent and why]* … my foot went down and pop that's all I heard pop

The build was ~500 words. The break was one syllable.

**Why it works:** the reader has settled into the ordinary before it goes. That's what makes it land, not the intensity of the description.

**In an article:** open a section on the boring version of the reader's day. The commute, the routine appointment, the thing they've done a hundred times. Then break it in one short sentence. Never signpost the break with "but then everything changed."

---

## 2. Name the state, then interrogate your own name for it

> Self-imposed isolation. What do I mean by that? Well, exactly what it says on the tin.

He names an abstract state, immediately questions his own phrase, then answers plainly. This is **not** §2 Move 2, which asks a diagnostic question of the reader or the subject. This one is turned on himself, and it works as an opener for anything abstract.

**In an article:** use it to open a section on something with no visible symptom. Name it, ask what you mean by it, then define it in plain terms. Two sentences, then you're into the substance.

---

## 3. A physical object for an invisible state

The most transferable device in the corpus, and the answer to writing symptoms without clinical language.

> Somebody had put a glass beaker over the top of me and I could see out and they could see in. We could both see each other, but we just had no physical contact or emotional contact.
>
> It's like looking at old photographs of yourself … but in this case there is no old photograph.
>
> it's like somebody's opened up a door and just said, okay, if you walk through door A, you can get back to that person in the distance … if you go through door B, well, it just all stays the same.

Every one reaches for a concrete object the reader can picture: a beaker, a photograph, a door. None reaches for a clinical or emotional abstraction.

**In an article:** when you need to convey a felt state, find the object. Never write "reduced sense of wellbeing" or "emotional flatness." Find the beaker.

**Compliance note:** the object stays a description of feeling. The moment it implies mechanism or outcome, it's a claim. Pre-flight it.

**Not to be confused with the §9a personification ban.** `tone-of-voice.md` §9a bans an inanimate subject doing a human verb. That ban targets *decorative* personification, where a vague verb hides the fact that no one is doing anything: "the marker tells a story", "the results reveal", "the decision emerges". It does **not** target personification that is the argument itself. "The range was built to answer one question" and "the framework isn't lying, it's answering a different question" are precise, falsifiable, and load-bearing; the sentence collapses without them. Test: can you name the human actor and keep the meaning? If yes, name them. If the inanimate thing genuinely is the actor in the claim you're making, leave it.

---

## 4. Show the search for the word

> I don't even know if fear is the right word, but it replaces you
>
> I think it was October 2018. No, sorry, October 2019.
>
> I don't know, I don't want to get too philosophical about it

He leaves the correction in. He admits the word isn't quite right and uses it anyway. Polished prose removes exactly this, and removing it is most of what makes writing sound machine-generated.

**In an article:** keep one visible hesitation per piece where the subject is genuinely uncertain. Not performed uncertainty about facts, which reads as hedging. Uncertainty about *naming* something.

---

## 5. Timestamp the vantage point

> But it's only looking back on it now at 2025.
>
> I can look back at it now in 2025, in December, and I think it's more like a kind of slow burn

He separates the man it happened to from the man telling it, out loud, with a date. This is what licenses the diagnostic-observer voice from `tone-of-voice.md` §1 without it turning detached: the observer is him, later, not a narrator standing outside.

**In an article:** when recounting anything retrospective, mark where you're standing. One clause is enough.

---

## 6. The banal scene carries the load

Never the summary of a symptom. Always one specific ordinary scene with a named trigger.

> every morning I would take my daughter to school, drive her to school and then on the way back … somebody had cut me up in the car … I just fly into a fit of rage … hitting the steering wheel

Not "I experienced mood swings." The school run, the indicator, the steering wheel.

**In an article:** every state gets a scene. If a paragraph describes how something feels and contains no specific object, place, or time, it isn't finished.

---

## 7. Understatement at the point of maximum weight

> I was now an invalid
>
> I felt like I had aged 10 years
>
> Yeah, pretty shit really.

The heaviest moments get the plainest words and the shortest sentences. He never escalates the language to match the event, which is why the events land.

**In an article:** at the emotional peak, cut adjectives rather than adding them. If a sentence is doing the most work in a section, it should be the shortest one in it.

---

## 8. Flat close

> there you go that's that's the achilles heel story
>
> Yeah, that's the music story.
>
> Yeah, pretty shit really.
>
> I just wonder.

No bow, no resolution, no rhetorical prompt. Where he does turn outward, it's phrased as his own musing rather than a question aimed at the reader:

> I just wonder how many men in the UK around the world feel the same, but are unable to … do anything about it

**This qualifies `tone-of-voice.md` §9**, which requires every draft to close with a question to the reader. That rule holds for LinkedIn, where the question earns a comment. In long-form it produces a uniform, slightly salesy ending on every article. In articles, a flat close or an open wondering is in voice and is often stronger. Treat the §9 closing-question box as satisfied by any of the three.

---

## 9. First person throughout, widen only at the end

He stays inside his own experience for the entire passage and generalises only in the closing lines, if at all. He never opens on the general claim and narrows to himself.

**In an article:** resist the instinct to open with "many men find that…". Start inside one experience. Earn the generalisation, then spend it once, at the end.

## The structural audit

Devices 1 to 9 supply shape at the paragraph and passage level. This section
works one level up, on the skeleton of a whole piece and on how that skeleton
compares to the last few pieces published in the same channel.

It exists because `tone-of-voice.md` operates almost entirely on words and
sentences (banned vocabulary, the em dash ban, the six §9a tells) and a draft
can pass every one of those boxes and still read as machine-made. The evidence
that shape is the durable signal comes from StoryScope (Russell et al., 2026,
[arXiv 2604.03136](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03136)): discourse-level narrative
features alone separate human from AI text at 93.2% macro-F1, and professional
stylistic rewriting moved detection by 1.6 points. AI writing clusters in one
tight region of structural space; human writing scatters. **Rarity is the
signal.**

Two calibration warnings before using any of it. The StoryScope corpus is
~5,000-word prompted fiction, so the percentages below are direction, not a
threshold for UK health copy. And the audits are diagnostic only: where an audit
collides with a house device, §9a's carve-out table wins.

### The six audits

Run these against the **skeleton**, not the prose. Structural tells are invisible
at reading distance because good sentences hide them.

**1. Theme explicitness.** AI states its lesson 77% of the time against 52% for
humans. State the central point once, where it lands hardest, and delete every
restatement. Leave at least one example uninterpreted.

> This is the audit most likely to fire on our articles, because Move 4 ("It's
> not X. It's Y.") is a mandatory lesson statement and §9 requires one per draft.
> One per draft is right. One per H2 is the 77% behaviour with our fingerprints
> on it. Measured 2026-08-10: most sections in the three most recent articles
> close on a restated thesis. **Move 4 stays; the per-section repeat goes.**

**2. Structural tidiness.** AI runs unbroken causal chains 79% of the time
against 57% for humans; humans digress and loop far more (tangential parallels
42% against 21%). Pick one: a loosely related tangent, one question you name and
do not answer, or stopping before full resolution.

> Compliance constrains this more than the source assumes. An unanswered
> question about a marker's meaning is fine; an unanswered question about
> whether the reader should act is not. Route the tangent through something
> observational rather than clinical.

**3. Emotion mode.** AI performs emotion through the body 81% of the time
against 38% for humans (chest tightening, breath catching). Humans label
feelings plainly. Name the feeling; reserve embodied detail for one earned
moment.

> Compatible with device 7 (understatement at maximum weight) and stronger when
> stacked with it. Not currently a live problem in our corpus.

**4. Reference specificity.** Humans name real entities 47% of the time against
24%. Replace "an expert" with a name, "recently" with a date, a generic item
with the actual brand, figure, or place.

> Already satisfied. `tone-of-voice.md` §3 and the compliance citation rules
> cover it, and a scan of all 18 published articles on 2026-08-10 found zero
> instances of vague attribution. Audit retained for completeness; expect it to
> pass.

**5. Reader engagement.** Humans address the reader directly 28% of the time
against 7%, and break the fourth wall more often (67% against 39%). One sparing
admission that somebody is reading this.

> Already satisfied, and this is one where the house rules are on the human side
> of the distribution. Move 5 and the second-person register do the work. Do not
> add more.

**6. Shape convergence.** Repetition of skeleton across recent pieces is the
single strongest signal, because it is the mechanism behind all of the above.
Compare the draft's skeleton against the last three pieces in the same channel
and break whatever repeats before publishing.

> **This is the live failure and the reason this section exists.** Measured
> across the 18 published articles on 2026-08-10:
>
> | Repeated element | Frequency |
> | --- | --- |
> | `## Your next move` as the closing H2 | 18 of 18 |
> | `## What changes when you actually have the number` | 7 of 18, verbatim |
> | `## Why your GP ordered it, or why your panel includes it` | 5 of 18, verbatim |
> | `## How Andro Prime measures [marker]` | 10 of 18 |
>
> The `no repeated openers` rule in `tone-of-voice.md` §3 catches the first
> sentence and nothing else. Every article in the library shares a closing slot,
> and more than half share two or three interior slots in the same order. A
> template CTA block is a deliberate product decision and stays; the *editorial*
> H2s around it are not, and they need to rotate.

### The intervention menu

Reach for one or two per piece. Never run the list.

- **Outcome first.** Open at the endpoint, then rewind for context.
- **Cold open mid-scene.** Start inside the action, loop back later.
- **Delayed reveal.** Withhold the key number or name until two-thirds through.
- **Recontextualisation callback.** Reframe an earlier detail with new meaning
  midway.
- **The oblique tangent.** One unresolved parallel paragraph.
- **The open thread.** Name a question and leave it unanswered.
- **Genuine ambivalence.** End holding two things at once.
- **The named thing.** Replace every vague allusion with a verifiable specific.
- **Plain emotion.** State the feeling instead of performing it physically.
- **Acknowledged reader.** One moment admitting somebody reads this.
- **End hot.** Stop at the spike; cut the quiet coda.

Several of these are devices 1 to 9 under different names, which is expected:
the menu is the rotation set, the devices are the technique.

### The rotation rule

**Never apply all the fixes at once. Pick one or two interventions per piece,
vary them across pieces, and be able to say why this piece got this shape.**

This is the rule the rest of the section depends on, and the one the spec has
already been taught twice by failure rather than by design. Both times the fix
became the tell:

- The verbatim "I asked him one question" opener propagated to most live
  articles and was retired (`tone-of-voice.md` §2 Move 2).
- A diagnostic question in every H2 produced the formula feel and was qualified
  ("What this corpus does not support", below).

Both are the same disease at different altitudes, and the shape-convergence
table above is the third instance. A prescription applied uniformly is
indistinguishable from a template no matter how good the prescription is.
**Any rule in the voice spec that mandates a repeatable unit is subject to this
rule, including the ones in `tone-of-voice.md`.** Specifically: §6 instructs
that each H2 is one arc of hook, question, reframe, close. Read that as the
default arc available to a section, not as a shape every section must take. If
every H2 in a draft has the same skeleton, the draft has failed audit 6
regardless of how well the sentences read.

## What this corpus does not support

Two rules in `tone-of-voice.md` are not evidenced in natural speech and appear to be artefacts of the written LinkedIn samples:

- **The mandatory closing question** (§9). See device 8.
- **A diagnostic question in every section** (§9). In the spoken corpus, questions arrive when he's genuinely working something out, roughly once per chapter, not on a per-section schedule. Forcing one per H2 is what produces the "formula" feel the spec was written to avoid.

Both §9 checklist boxes have been qualified in place (2026-07-27) rather than
left for v2, because `/article` reads them on every draft and the stricter
reading was actively producing the formula feel. The boxes still exist; they
now accept the spoken-corpus forms.
